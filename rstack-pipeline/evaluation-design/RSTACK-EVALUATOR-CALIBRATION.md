# RSTACK Evaluator Calibration

## Purpose

This document defines how an AI-as-judge evaluator should be tested before its labels are used to guide RSTACK changes.

The goal is not to prove that an evaluator is "smart."

The goal is to establish:

- which judgments it can make reliably,
- where it produces false positives or false negatives,
- how stable it is across model/rubric changes,
- and when human adjudication remains necessary.

AI judge output is evidence, not ground truth.

---

# 1. Calibrate specific tasks, not "the evaluator"

Calibration should be task-specific.

Examples:

- classify a Planner claim as SUPPORTED / CONTRADICTED / UNSUPPORTED,
- classify an Auditor finding as TRUE_POSITIVE / FALSE_POSITIVE / NON_MATERIAL / UNRESOLVED,
- classify finding type,
- assess materiality,
- assess whether a test meaningfully proves an AC.

Do not assume that strong performance on claim classification transfers to test adequacy or architecture review.

---

# 2. Calibration inputs

Use a human-reviewed corpus containing:

- positive and negative cases,
- clean cases with no defect,
- ambiguous cases,
- multiple failure types,
- multiple materiality levels,
- and cases from more than one repository/task shape when allowed.

Prefer the golden evaluation corpus.

Do not calibrate only against cases generated by the same model being evaluated.

---

# 3. Preserve evaluator configuration

Every calibration run should identify:

- evaluator model/provider,
- model version if observable,
- evaluator prompt/config fingerprint,
- rubric version,
- tool/evidence access,
- host,
- date,
- corpus version,
- decoding/reasoning settings if observable.

If any of these materially change, consider the evaluator a new configuration that requires re-calibration.

---

# 4. Blind evaluation

Where practical, the evaluator should not see:

- the human gold label,
- prior evaluator labels,
- the experiment arm,
- whether the case was historically a known failure,
- or which model/agent produced the subject.

The objective is to judge the evidence, not confirm an expected narrative.

---

# 5. Classification metrics

For closed classification tasks with complete human labels, useful measurements may include:

- per-label precision,
- per-label recall,
- macro/micro F1 where appropriate,
- confusion matrix,
- unresolved/abstention rate,
- agreement with human labels.

Do not collapse these into one score if the error types have different consequences.

Example:

A judge that rarely produces false positives but misses many material defects may be useful for one purpose and unsuitable for another.

---

# 6. Discovery-task warning

Open-ended review is different from classifying a fixed set of claims.

For tasks such as:

> "Find all material defects in this plan"

recall cannot be claimed unless the gold set is reasonably complete.

Use terms such as:

- validated findings discovered,
- unique validated findings,
- false-positive findings,
- known gold findings missed,

and state the limits of the gold set.

Do not report "90% recall" from an incomplete list of known defects.

---

# 7. Materiality calibration

Evaluate materiality separately from validity.

A judge may correctly detect an issue but exaggerate its impact.

Track confusion between:

- CRITICAL,
- HIGH,
- MEDIUM,
- LOW,
- UNRESOLVED.

For high-stakes categories, inspect examples rather than relying only on aggregate metrics.

---

# 8. Abstention is allowed

A calibrated evaluator should be allowed to return:

- UNRESOLVED,
- insufficient evidence,
- or human decision required.

Do not optimize it to always choose a side.

Measure:

- unnecessary abstention,
- correct abstention,
- confident error.

Confident errors are especially useful for improving rubrics and evidence packaging.

---

# 9. Multi-judge disagreement

If multiple AI judges are used, disagreement does not automatically mean the minority judge is wrong.

Record each judgment separately.

Possible uses of disagreement:

- trigger human review,
- identify ambiguous rubric language,
- identify weak evidence,
- discover model-specific bias.

Do not majority-vote away material ambiguity without inspecting evidence.

---

# 10. Human adjudication

Human adjudication should record:

- final label,
- evidence used,
- whether the rubric was sufficient,
- whether the case itself was ambiguous,
- whether evaluator error came from reasoning, missing context, or rubric interpretation.

Preserve original evaluator outputs.

Do not rewrite history after the human label is known.

---

# 11. Calibration error taxonomy

When the evaluator disagrees with human adjudication, classify the cause where possible:

- evidence omitted from evaluator context,
- evidence misunderstood,
- requirement ambiguity,
- rubric ambiguity,
- label-boundary disagreement,
- evaluator hallucination,
- snapshot mismatch,
- materiality exaggeration,
- materiality understatement,
- host/tool observation unavailable.

The remedy depends on the cause.

Do not always respond by expanding the evaluator prompt.

---

# 12. Acceptance policy

Do not hard-code universal numeric thresholds before collecting baseline evidence.

For each evaluator use case, define:

- tolerated false-positive risk,
- tolerated false-negative risk,
- required human sampling,
- whether the evaluator may make an automated decision or only provide evidence.

Examples:

## Low-risk analytics

An evaluator may be acceptable for trend analysis even with imperfect individual labels if uncertainty is reported and samples are human-checked.

## Pipeline-changing decisions

Require stronger calibration and human review before accepting conclusions that alter RSTACK behavior.

## Release/security/authorization

Do not let an LLM judge replace deterministic controls or required human authority.

---

# 13. Drift monitoring

Recalibrate when:

- evaluator model changes,
- evaluator prompt/rubric changes,
- evidence packaging changes materially,
- host/tool access changes,
- new failure categories appear,
- human disagreement rate rises,
- or periodic review shows distribution shift.

Compare the same fixed calibration subset across evaluator versions to detect drift.

# 15. What calibration must not become

Do not turn calibration into:

- a contest for the highest single score,
- repeated prompt tuning against the held-out set,
- an excuse to remove human validation,
- or a mechanism for making the evaluator agree with the current pipeline.

The evaluator exists to help discover truth about RSTACK, not to confirm that RSTACK is good.
